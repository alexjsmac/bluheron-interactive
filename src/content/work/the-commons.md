The Commons turned the tragedy of the commons into something you can stand
inside. For one night of Lumen 2026, an underground parking garage beneath
Waterloo City Centre held a shared atmosphere of light and four-channel sound,
and the condition of that atmosphere was decided by whoever happened to be in
the room.

Every visitor was given a voice and a column of light of their own. Hands held
low kept the room in balance: a glowing, generative soundscape that belonged to
everyone present. Every raised hand made the whole thing brighter, wider and
rougher, for everybody. Nobody could break it alone, and nobody could fix it
alone. The empty room was the most beautiful version of the piece, and each
person who walked in made it a little more complicated.

The design choice that mattered most was the slow recovery. When the room tips
into noise, it takes much longer to calm down than it took to get loud, and it
only comes back when the whole room lowers its hands. Damage is quick and repair is slow, which is
the most honest thing the piece has to say about emissions.

Lumen draws a big crowd, so the piece had to work without a queue. Body
tracking let a whole group shape the sound together instead of taking turns,
and the light and sound spilled out far enough that people walking past got
something too. The garage had nothing to hang lights from, so the LED columns
stood on their own frames, and the low concrete ceiling became part of the
light show.
